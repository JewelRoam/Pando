# Research Round 135 — The accelerators drop out under concurrency

Date: 2026-10-15

## What round 134 left to build

Round 134 concluded that a fleet should prefer the member that is cheapest to
the machine it lives on, and that `pick_quorum` had no idea what a member
costs. This round adds the signal and the rule, and then finds that the
numbers the rule was built on cannot be trusted.

## The change

- `pandosi/stemd.py`: a stem measures its own **host CPU per decision**
  (`process_time()` over the decisions it has served, so it is measured and
  not claimed) and reports it in `/health` and on **every heartbeat** - a stem
  that has served nothing yet has no cost to report, so a one-shot
  registration would have carried `null` forever.
- `pandosi/rootd.py`: `pick_quorum` sorts within a checkpoint group by
  **cost band** first (ten-fold buckets), then by in-flight calls. Bucketing
  rather than strict ordering is the point: two units within a factor of ten
  are peers and share the load - the property rounds 109-110 established -
  while a unit orders of magnitude more expensive is not woken while a cheap
  one is free. A member that reports no cost lands in the last bucket, so
  every earlier round's behaviour is unchanged.

Measured on a mixed fleet of one lineage (CPU / iGPU / NPU stems reporting
6734 / 47 / 47 ms of host CPU per decision): the CPU stem's share of served
decisions fell from about a third under the old rotation to 13%, and in a
clean 20-decision run to **zero** - the picker routed around the expensive
member and split the work between the two accelerators.

## The field that found the real problem

Many decisions still came back `escalate` with `quorum_size: 1` and
`responded: 0`: a member was picked and said nothing, which looks to a client
exactly like a member that was never asked. `decide` now reports
**`unanswered`** - the members that were picked and dropped out - and that
one field immediately repriced the whole round:

| member | served | silent (idle phase) | silent (loaded phase) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| CPU stem | 15 | **0** | **0** |
| iGPU stem | 35 | 31 | 18 |
| NPU stem | 17 | 22 | 34 |

**The accelerators drop out under concurrency and the CPU stem never does.**
Asked one task at a time, the same NPU stem answers 10 of 10 distinct tasks
in 105-176 ms with no failures. At four concurrent clients the OpenVINO
stems fail roughly half their calls; the ONNX Runtime CPU stem survives the
same load untouched. The likely mechanism is the obvious one - one OpenVINO
infer-request object shared across handler threads is not thread-safe - and
`MockBackend` already carries a `serialize` flag for exactly this reason,
with the comment that a real device serves one decision at a time.

## What is withdrawn

Round 134's numbers for the accelerators were measured without knowing which
responses were real. Its 171-341 decisions/s and 1.4-1.8 ms of host CPU per
decision for the iGPU and NPU are the dropout artifact: a silent member is
fast and costs nothing, and its `stem_cpu` reading of ~1.5 ms/dec is the
fingerprint of a member that did almost no work. The claims about the CPU
stem stand - it never drops out - but the ratio they were compared against
does not.

What survives from rounds 130-134 about the units: their **sequential**
behaviour, which was measured one request at a time (CPU ~94 ms, iGPU
~53 ms, NPU ~112 ms per decision; identical answers on 240/240 tasks), and
the structural findings about quorum size (k multiplies latency) and
replication (units of one lineage are one opinion).

## The next step, now that it is visible

A device should serve one decision at a time. That is what `serialize`
already models, what the round-109 measurements assumed, and what the
accelerators require. With that in place the cost signal becomes meaningful
again - and this time the harness can tell a served decision from a silent
one, which is the only reason this round could find the problem at all.
