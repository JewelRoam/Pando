# Research Round 117 — Does the swarm decide better than its best member?

Date: 2026-10-15

## The question nobody had asked

Everything in this project rests on one premise: that several decision
models assembled into a quorum make *better* decisions than one of them
alone. Rounds 57 and 99 measured that a checkpoint-diverse quorum produces
independent evidence, and rounds 99-100 measured that the gate lifts
accuracy on the work it accepts. Neither is the premise. Neither compares
the swarm against a single member given the same amount of work.

That is the comparison this round makes, on the 27 labeled choice tasks,
with the production calibration applied and three real lineages running on
CPU: at matched coverage, is the assembled swarm better than the strongest
single lineage allowed to pick its own most-confident tasks?

## A bug in the first version of the measurement

The first run reported all three lineages at exactly 51.85% - identical to
the swarm's majority vote. That was wrong, and the way it was wrong is
familiar from rounds 103 and 107: the per-lineage accuracy was computed from
the row's `correct` field, which describes **the swarm's** answer, not that
member's. Recounting from the raw rows gave 15, 16 and 15 of 27.

The corrected numbers are below. The combiner sweep never had the bug
because it recomputes each combination's own answer against the label.

At matched coverage the ensemble's lift is **-7.7 points** - one task.

## The combiner is the gap, not the members

Six ways of combining the same recorded decisions, ranked by how much
coverage they can hold at >=90% accuracy:

| combiner | accuracy at 13/27 | full coverage | holds >=90% to |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| `best_lineage` (one lineage's own confidence) | **100%** | 59.3% | 51.85% |
| `gate` (shipped) | 92.3% | 51.8% | 51.85% |
| `max_conf` (most confident member) | 92.3% | 55.6% | 51.85% |
| `min_conf` (least confident member) | 92.3% | **63.0%** | 51.85% |
| `margin` (weighted vote spread) | 92.3% | 51.8% | 48.15% |
| `agreement` (unanimity first) | 92.3% | 51.8% | 48.15% |

Two things stand out. Every combination of members is beaten at the
incumbent's operating point by just using the best member's confidence, and
the majority vote does not even match the members. And the one combiner that
beats all members at *full* coverage is ordering decisions by the **least**
confident member - 63.0% against 59.3% for the best single model - which is
the opposite of the intuition the gate encodes.

## What is established, and what is not

Established:

- on this suite the majority vote adds no accuracy over a single member, and
  the members' 55.6-59.3% spread survives the vote;
- the gate is not the best available selector: one lineage's own confidence
  order holds 90% or better across the same coverage and reaches 13/13;
- the split signal is real and strong (16.7% when members disagree against
  80.0% when they agree), so *refusing* remains the swarm's measurable
  contribution;
- complementary knowledge exists - the correct answer is present in the trio
  on 20 of 27 tasks - and the current combiners capture none of the 22-point
  gap to the oracle.

Not established, and this matters: **27 tasks cannot decide between 52% and
63%.** A 95% interval on 16/27 spans roughly +/-18 points, so every
difference in the tables above is one to three tasks. The honest statement is
that the swarm's accuracy advantage is *unverified*, that a single member's
confidence is at least as good a selector as the gate on this data, and that
the vote-costs-accuracy effect is a hypothesis worth testing properly rather
than a finding.

## What this changes

The project's demonstrated value so far is availability, elasticity and
refusal: a swarm keeps deciding when devices join and leave, grows and
shrinks itself, and knows when to abstain. Its accuracy claim is the part
that is not yet standing on evidence, and the measurement, not the mechanism,
is what is missing.

## Next step

Scale the labeled suite from 27 tasks to several hundred before touching the
combiner. The repository already has the machinery
(`tools/expand_suite.py`, `tools/build_prompt_pool.py`, and the RLCD contrast
rows); the cheapest path is to generate templated intent/action decisions
with unambiguous expected answers, keep a held-out split, and re-run
`tools/ensemble_value_check.py` and `tools/combiner_check.py`. With a few
hundred tasks the two candidates that matter - the shipped gate and the
least-confident-member selector - can be separated, and if the vote still
loses to its members the honest response is to fix the combiner rather than
add devices.
