# Research Round 137 — Independence is the admission test; the rest is noise

Date: 2026-10-15

## The rule the elastic premise needs

Rounds 122-129 established by measurement that a swarm buys *independence*
and not accuracy: a 57.5%-accurate decorrelated member was worth several
points, a 76%-accurate aligned one was worth less, four correlated members
cost 11.6 points, and a replica is worth nothing. Each of those was one
comparison between members that differed in several ways at once.

This round builds a portfolio in which the two candidate predictors vary
independently - ten cheap members from three feature views (word/bigram,
character trigram, both) at three smoothing strengths plus a nearest-centroid
classifier - and measures each one's accuracy, its mean error correlation
with the three neural lineages, how often it is right when the trio is wrong
("rescues"), how often it is wrong when the trio is right ("harms"), and what
it actually adds to the swarm's full-coverage accuracy on the 240
wording-held-out rows.

## What the numbers say

**Every member of the portfolio is decorrelated** - mean phi between -0.06
and +0.08 - and nine of the ten add a positive amount. That is the finding
that matters at the family level, and it is consistent with rounds 122-129:
the axis those rounds measured was a *family* difference (same-family errors
correlate at +0.2 to +0.8, outside-family at about zero), and it is reproduced
here in ten independent draws.

**Within the decorrelated family, none of the three statistics predicts the
value.** Rank correlation against the measured gain: accuracy **-0.55**, mean
phi **-0.02**, rescues-minus-harms **-0.55**. The most accurate members are
*not* the most valuable ones - the two members with 60-62% accuracy sit in the
middle and the bottom of the table, and the least accurate member tested
(centroid, 42.9%) is the best. The mechanism quantity fails too: `nb-chars`
rescues the trio 30-32 times against `nb-words`' 18-21, and still does not add
more, because a member's rescues only reach the outcome on rows where the trio
splits, and the arithmetic of a four-member gate mediates the rest.

**And the spread is noise anyway.** The gains run from -1 to +8 decisions out
of 240, which is the same order as the sampling error at this size; the sign
of the middle of the table is not a fact about the members.

## The rule that survives, and what it costs

The usable admission test is the coarse one: **a candidate earns a seat by
being decorrelated from the members already seated**, and nothing beyond that
predicts what it will be worth at a few hundred rows. A fleet should therefore
admit on independence, measure the rest after the fact - which the project's
own replay machinery does cheaply - and not spend effort choosing between
members that pass the test.

This also answers the question round 129 left open in a more useful way than
a finer rule would have: if the fine structure within a decorrelated family
cannot be resolved at 240 rows, then a bloc's representation rule cannot be
either, and the honest default is the one that is cheapest to explain.
