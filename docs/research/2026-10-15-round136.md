# Research Round 136 — A device serves one decision at a time, and the numbers come back

Date: 2026-10-15

## The fix round 135 asked for

Round 135 found the accelerators failing about half their calls under four
concurrent clients while the ONNX CPU stem never failed, and named the likely
mechanism: one OpenVINO infer-request shared across handler threads.
`MockBackend` had carried the answer all along - a `serialize` flag, with the
comment that *a real device serves one decision at a time*.

`Stem` now serialises by default: every stem holds a lock around
`backend.decide`, with `stemd --parallel` as the opt-out for a backend that
has proven itself thread-safe. The default is honest, and the opt-out is a
claim the operator has to make.

## Verification: the dropouts are gone

The same mixed fleet - CPU, iGPU and NPU stems of one lineage, four
concurrent clients, 120 decisions per phase - with the serialising stems:

| phase | served by | silent |
|---|---|---|
| idle | `{en-gpu: 66, en-npu: 54}` | **0** |
| loaded | `{en-npu: 120}` | **0** |

Before the fix the same run had 49-56 silent decisions out of 120. Now every
decision is served, the expensive CPU stem is never chosen (it reports 6578 ms
of host CPU per decision against the accelerators' 52 and 31), idle work
splits between the two accelerators, and under load the fleet converges on
the NPU - the cheapest member for the machine.

## The unit table, corrected

Rounds 134-135 withdrew their accelerator figures because a silent member
looks fast and free. With serialising stems the client-observed latency is
`4 x service time` (four queued clients) and the throughput is
`1 / service time`, so the service times below are consistent in both
columns for the first time:

| unit | service time, host idle | service time, host loaded | host CPU per decision (loaded) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| CPU | ~94 ms | **~309 ms** | **2856 ms** |
| Intel iGPU | ~53 ms | **~35 ms** | 36 ms |
| Intel NPU | ~112 ms | **~109 ms** | **4 ms** |

The three findings that matter, all measured with a harness that now knows
the difference between a served decision and a silent one:

1. **The CPU stem degrades by 3.3x when the host is loaded** (94 -> 309 ms of
   service time) and devours **2.9 core-seconds of host CPU per decision**.
   The user's premise - that the CPU is the part that is always busy - shows
   up here as a device fighting the machine for cores.
2. **The accelerators do not degrade at all** (the iGPU even measures faster,
   53 -> 35 ms, consistent with its steady state being under the idle-phase
   figure). They are available exactly when the machine is in its normal
   state.
3. **The cost ratio is 80-700x**: 4 ms (NPU) and 36 ms (iGPU) of host CPU per
   decision against 2856 ms (CPU). Not the 500-1000x of the withdrawn round
   134, but the same conclusion with numbers that survive.

## What a fleet does with this

At k=1 the cost-aware picker now runs the fleet at ~21 decisions/s idle and
~9/s loaded, entirely on the accelerators, and it announces which members
dropped out when any do. That is the shape the elastic premise needs: the
devices that are free are the ones that serve, the expensive one is left idle
for the user, and the response says who actually voted.
