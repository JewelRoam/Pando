# Research Round 118 — The ensemble question at 480 tasks, and why little is left to combine

Date: 2026-10-15

## Why a bigger suite

Round 117's verdict on the swarm premise was "unverified, not refuted": 27
labeled tasks cannot separate a 52% combiner from a 63% one. The answer to a
question that size is not a better argument, it is more tasks. This round
builds a suite whose labels are true by construction and runs it on two
independent splits.

## A suite whose labels cannot be argued with

`tools/build_large_suite.py` generates choice tasks over eight deliberately
confusable intents (refund / chargeback / exchange / complaint / praise /
greeting / login_issue / invoice). Every row carries exactly one class cue;
the difficulty comes from paraphrase, colloquial forms and typo'd surface
text, plus filler sentences that carry no class vocabulary at all.

The filler neutrality is *asserted*, not trusted: the builder matches
fillers against class-distinctive words on whole tokens. The first version
matched substrings and passed a filler that mentioned the receipt, which is
an invoice cue - the assertion caught it, and the filler was rewritten. That
is the entire reason the assertion exists.

Two seeds produce the splits: `assets/big_dev.jsonl` (seed 1, 240 rows) and
`assets/big_test.jsonl` (seed 2, 240 rows). Task ids do not overlap. 79 of
the test rows reuse a dev row's sentence, and all 79 agree with dev on the
label, so the reuse costs nothing but is worth knowing when reading the
test numbers as held-out. The generator never sees a model.

## The leaderboard was comparing against the wrong member

`tools/combiner_check.py` scored six combiners and one `best_lineage` row.
That row was the *reference* lineage the project had been measuring against,
not the best member of the trio - and on the new suite the reference
(`multilingual`) is the **weakest** of the three. The row was mislabelled,
and mislabelling it is exactly what made round 117 look as if the ensemble
lost to "the best member" by seven points.

The tool now puts every member on the leaderboard in its own right
(`member:<lineage>`) and picks the strongest one automatically, so the
question "does the swarm beat its best member" is asked of the actual best
member. `tools/ensemble_significance.py` was added alongside it for the
paired tests and the power estimate.

## The ensemble's edge is small, and it has the right sign

Pooled over the two splits, `margin` reaches 89.6% against the best member's
88.5%. A paired bootstrap of that difference, re-ranking inside each of 2000
resamples:

| comparison point | difference | 95% CI | positive in |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| full coverage (480 tasks) | +1.04 pt | [-1.04, +3.12] | 81.5% of draws |
| the gate's coverage (383 tasks) | +1.62 pt | [0.00, +3.39] | 96.4% of draws |

So: at the operating point the project actually ships, the assembled swarm
is better than its best member by about a point and a half, and it is barely
distinguishable from zero. At full coverage it is one point, and
indistinguishable. Split by split the two disagree on sign - read on the
shipped gate against the best member at the same coverage, dev is -0.5 pt
and test is +1.1 pt - which is what a true effect of about +1 point looks
like at n=240 per split.

This is the first round in which the swarm premise is *supported* rather
than unverified. It is supported weakly, and the next section says why the
weakness is structural rather than a shortage of data.

## The combiner earns its point on disagreements, and leaves 17 on the table

157 of the 480 rows (32.7%) are rows where the trio does not agree. On those
rows:

| resolution rule | accuracy on disagreements |
|---|---:|
| correct answer present somewhere in the trio | **91.7%** (144/157) |
| `margin` (weighted vote) | 74.5% |
| `member:typed-decisions` | 71.3% |
| plain majority vote | 70.7% |
| `gate` (shipped) | 70.7% |
| `max_conf` | 65.6% |
| `member:english` | 61.1% |
| `min_conf` (veto) | 51.6% |
| `member:multilingual` | 29.9% |

The weighted vote is the only rule that beats a plain count, and it beats it
by 6 rows. That is the whole of the ensemble's advantage. Above it sit 27
rows where the right answer was already in the trio and every rule still
discarded it.

`min_conf` is worth a note: at n=27 it looked like the best combiner in the
project (63.0% at full coverage). On disagreements it is 51.6% - a coin
flip. Round 117 was careful enough to call it a curiosity; it was noise.

Round 57b, on its own 40-task suite, put the same statistic at 36% and drew
a strong conclusion from it: "auto-executing split decisions would be
dangerous - 64% error rate". The split *rate* replicates closely (35% there,
32.7% here), the split *reliability* does not (36% there, 70.7% here). Both
can be true: round 57's suite sits in the 55-59% accuracy regime and this one
in the 88% regime, and a rule that is right two times in three when its
members are barely better than chance is not the same rule. What does not
survive is the reading that 36% is a property of the swarm rather than of
the 14 tasks it was measured on.

## Why there is so little to combine: the members share their errors

The three admitted lineages are prompt variants of the same weights, and
their errors arrive together:

| pair | disagreement | both wrong, vs independence | phi |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| `english` x `typed-decisions` | 11.7% | **x4.30** | +0.495 |
| `multilingual` x `typed-decisions` | 23.1% | x2.33 | +0.276 |
| `english` x `multilingual` | 25.2% | x1.97 | +0.234 |

If the errors were independent at these rates, a majority of the trio would
be wrong 7.4% of the time. It is wrong 11.7% of the time - 1.6x the
independent-error prediction. The premise that makes three mediocre members
beat one good one is exactly the premise that fails here.

## The number that decides the next experiment

`margin` and `typed-decisions` answer correctly-or-not differently on 5.6% of
tasks. That discordance rate, not the size of the suite, is what limits
resolution:

| difference to resolve | at 5% discordance | 10% | 20% | 30% |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 point | 3,925 | 7,849 | 15,698 | 23,547 |
| 2 points | 982 | 1,963 | 3,925 | 5,887 |
| 5 points | 157 | 314 | 628 | 942 |

(tasks for 80% power at alpha = 0.05)

Buying more of the *same* tasks costs linearly in compute and buys resolution
as the square root. Buying more *diversity* moves every cell: at 30%
discordance the 2-point column drops by 6x. The next round should spend its
compute on making the members different, not on generating a third split of
the same eight intents.

## What this does and does not say

- It does not say the swarm works. A one-point edge measured with a
  confidence interval that touches zero is a direction, not a result.
- It does say the disagreement is where the value is, that the shipped
  weighted vote is the best available rule there, and that the correct
  answer is present in 91.7% of disagreements while the rule captures 74.5%.
  That 17-point gap is the project's actual remaining headroom.
- It does say the admission list matters 13x more than the combiner. The
  trio spans 13.5 accuracy points; every combiner in the sweep spans 1.
- The suite is easier than round 117's hand-built one (best member 88.5%
  pooled against 59.3% there), so the regime has changed. The result is
  measured in a high-accuracy regime; the low-accuracy regime remains n=27
  and undecided.

## Correction (round 122)

The two splits draw from one phrasing inventory: 53 distinct cues appear in
each, all 53 are shared, and every one of the 240 test rows uses a cue that
also appears in dev. The held-out split therefore measures generalisation to
unseen *pairings* of phrasings, not to unseen phrasings - a naive Bayes
fitted on dev reaches 93.75% on it, above every neural lineage. Nothing in
this round's *ensemble* finding depends on that (the comparison is between
members on the same rows), but every accuracy figure here should be read as
"unseen combinations", and a phrasing-held-out replacement now exists
(`assets/heldout_test.jsonl`). See `docs/research/2026-10-15-round122.md`.
