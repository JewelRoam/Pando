# Research Round 123 — What the swarm is worth on wording it has never seen

Date: 2026-10-15

## Fixing the instrument

Round 122 found that the dev and test splits shared their phrasing
inventory. The measurement made it exact: **53 distinct cues in each split,
53 shared, and every one of the 240 test rows uses a cue that also appears in
dev.** The "held-out" split was held out by pairing, not by wording.

`tools/build_large_suite.py` now carries a second inventory - 48 phrasings
for the same eight intents, written to appear in no earlier split - and
`assets/heldout_test.jsonl` is the 240-row suite built from it (seed 3,
`--cues b`). Verified against both older splits: **0 of 48 cues seen before,
0 of 240 rows reusing familiar wording.**

The generator's filler-neutrality assertion now checks every cue set rather
than the active one, and it caught two leaks while this inventory was being
written: a cue containing "message" and one containing "email", both of which
are filler vocabulary that would have made the filler informative.

## Every member loses ten to fifteen points

The same three lineages, the same task construction, only the wording new:

| member | shared-inventory split | wording-held-out split | change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| `english` | 85.00% | 75.42% | -9.6 |
| `multilingual` | 74.17% | 64.17% | -10.0 |
| `typed-decisions` | 87.08% | 72.50% | **-14.6** |
| majority vote | 87.50% | 73.75% | -13.8 |
| oracle (answer in the trio) | 94.17% | 84.58% | -9.6 |

The best member on the shared-inventory split was `typed-decisions`, by 2.1
points over `english`. On new wording it is `english`, by 2.9 points - and
`typed-decisions` is the member that loses the most. The two metrics disagree
though, which is worth keeping: ranked by confidence-curve coverage at >=90%,
`typed-decisions` still leads (70.8% of rows against 65.4%). So "which
lineage is strongest" is not a stable fact about the lineages; it is a
function of which wording distribution you ask about, and the previous
three-round picture (typed-decisions best by 2-5 points) was measured
partly in familiarity.

The gate holds up better than the raw accuracy suggests: it accepts 172/240
rows at 90.12% (against 187/240 at 97.86% on the shared split) - it gives up
coverage rather than accuracy, which is what it is for. The ensemble's
matched-coverage edge over the best member is +1.17 points here, against
+1.07 there: the swarm's small advantage survives new wording even as
everyone's absolute numbers fall.

## The outsiders from round 122 were recognising phrasing

Round 122's three foreign voters, re-scored on wording they have never seen:

| voter | shared-inventory split | wording-held-out split | note |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| `bayes` | 93.75% | **57.50%** | always answers |
| `rules` | 95.00% | **17.50%** | but 100% precise: 42/42 correct, 198 abstentions |
| `shadow` (control) | 98.75% | 76.25% | always answers |

Both outsiders collapse, and `rules` is the extreme case: 198 of 240 rows
match no keyword at all, and every one of the 42 rows it does answer is
correct. It is not a weak voter, it is a **selective** one, and scoring
abstention as error understates it badly. That is a scoring defect this round
found and did not fix: the four-member ensemble simply drops a None vote, so
"plus rules" means a three-member quorum on 198 rows.

So round 122's headline numbers do not survive: the outsiders' edge was
mostly vocabulary recognition, and the +3.2 matched-coverage points it
credited to decorrelation were earned at least partly by knowing the cue
list.

## What does survive is the mechanism

On the honest split the outsiders' accuracies now *bracket* the neural
members instead of exceeding them, which makes the control much sharper:

| voter | accuracy | mean phi with the trio | ensemble at matched coverage | at full coverage |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| three lineages alone | - | - | 85.47% | 77.08% |
| +`bayes` | 57.50% | **+0.07** | **89.53%** | **80.83%** |
| +`rules` (selective) | 17.50% / 100% precise | +0.08 | 86.05% | 77.92% |
| +`shadow` | **76.25%** | +0.39 | 86.05% | 74.58% |

The 57.50%-accurate decorrelated voter is worth **+4.1 points at matched
coverage and +3.8 at full coverage**. The 76.25%-accurate voter whose errors
were aligned with an existing member is worth less at matched coverage and
**-2.5 points at full coverage**. Accuracy ranks them `bayes < shadow`;
benefit ranks them `bayes > shadow`. That is round 122's conclusion, restated
on an instrument where it can be trusted, and with the accuracy gap pointing
the other way.

## What this means for the project

The swarm's premise has now been tested in the direction it needed:

- members that share a family, a checkpoint lineage, or an error structure
  add nothing, and a copy is worse than nothing (rounds 119, 121);
- a member whose errors are independent is worth several points even when it
  is 19 points *less* accurate than the rival candidate for the same seat
  (this round);
- no amount of combiner work substitutes for that (rounds 118, 120).

The practical consequence is that the membership question - "who is
different from us?" - is the project's main lever, and it now has an
instrument to answer it: a suite whose wording is genuinely unseen, and a
divergence table (`tools/foreign_voter_check.py`) that says how independent a
candidate is before it is admitted.
