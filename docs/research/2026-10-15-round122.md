# Research Round 122 — The ensemble buys decorrelation, not accuracy

Date: 2026-10-15

## The question the last three rounds converged on

Round 118 measured that no combiner over the same votes is worth more than a
point, round 119 that a same-family checkpoint adds nothing to combine (it
decided identically to its parent on 240 of 240 tasks), and round 120 that
asking a narrower question is worth 1.3 points. Every route to the ~18-point
gap on disagreements runs through the same wall: the three lineages' errors
arrive together, at phi +0.23 to +0.53.

So the premise's only untested form is "add a member whose errors do not
travel with the others". This round adds three voters from outside the neural
family to the recorded test-split votes - no neural model was re-run, so the
only thing that differs between columns is the fourth member:

| voter | what it is | test accuracy |
|---|---|---:|
| `bayes` | multinomial naive Bayes over tokens and bigrams, fitted on dev | 93.75% |
| `rules` | zero-shot keyword matching, nothing fitted | 95.00% |
| `shadow` | *control*: `typed-decisions` with its misses repaired by `rules` | **98.75%** |

`shadow` is the experiment's centre of gravity. It is at least as accurate as
`typed-decisions` by construction (its errors are a subset of
`typed-decisions`' errors, so its correlation with that member is positive
and its accuracy cannot be lower), and it borrows `rules`' answers
wholesale - so it holds the outsider's knowledge constant and varies only
*where* the errors sit.

## Result: the ranking of benefit follows correlation and inverts accuracy

Accuracy at the 187 rows the shipped gate accepts, and at full coverage:

| rule | three lineages | +`bayes` | +`rules` | +`shadow` |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| count majority | 96.26% / 87.50% | **100%** / 89.17% | **100%** / 89.58% | 99.47% / 88.75% |
| `margin` (weighted) | 96.26% / 88.75% | **100%** / 94.17% | **100%** / **95.00%** | 98.93% / 90.83% |
| `gate` (shipped) | 97.86% / 87.50% | **100%** / 89.17% | **100%** / 89.58% | 96.26% / 88.75% |

| voter | accuracy | mean phi with the trio | full-coverage ensemble |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| `rules` | 95.00% | **-0.014** | **95.00%** |
| `bayes` | 93.75% | +0.044 | 94.17% |
| `shadow` | 98.75% | +0.095 | 90.83% |

The voter with the **best** accuracy (`shadow`, 98.75%) produces the
**worst** ensemble. The voter with the **worst** accuracy (`bayes`, 93.75%)
beats it. Ranking the three by accuracy gives `shadow > rules > bayes`;
ranking them by the ensemble they produce gives `rules > bayes > shadow` -
the exact inverse. Ranking them by mean correlation with the trio gives
`rules < bayes < shadow`, which is the ensemble order.

Three points do not fit a law, and the voters differ in more than correlation.
What they do establish is that the ordering that would follow from accuracy is
refuted, and the ordering that follows from correlation is not - on members
whose knowledge of the task is held constant, because `shadow` is built out of
`rules`' own answers.

At matched coverage the number is stark: with either decorrelated outsider,
the weighted vote is **correct on all 187 rows** the shipped gate accepts,
against 96.26% for the trio and 96.79% for the best single member. Round 118
found a matched-coverage ensemble edge of about one point; the edge is +3.2
points here, and it comes entirely from the fourth member's independence.

## A defect this round found in the suite

`bayes` reaches 93.75% on the test split after being fitted on the dev split,
which is *above all three neural lineages* (85.0%, 74.2%, 87.1%). The reason
is not that naive Bayes is strong:

- dev and test draw cues from **one shared inventory of 53 phrasings** - 53
  distinct cues in each split, 53 shared;
- every one of the **240 test rows** uses a cue that also appears in dev;
- no cue carries conflicting labels across the splits, so the reuse is not
  even adversarial. It is the same sentence with a different filler.

So a learner fitted on dev is not generalising to new wording, it is
recognising wording it has seen. Round 118's "held-out" test split therefore
measures generalisation to unseen *pairings*, and its numbers should be read
that way. `tools/build_large_suite.py` now carries a second inventory
(`--cues b`) whose 48 phrasings appear in no earlier split, and
`assets/heldout_test.jsonl` is the suite built from it - 0 of its cues were
seen before, against 53 of 53 in the old test split.

This does not undo round 122's finding, for a reason worth stating: `shadow`
consumes `rules`' answers, so it has exactly the same vocabulary advantage as
`rules` and still fails to help, because its errors were aligned with an
existing member. The conclusion is about error placement, not about who knows
the cue list. But it does mean the suite needs a phrasing held-out split
before any accuracy number from it is used to compare a system against a
model.

## What this changes

The swarm premise is not refuted - it was measured in the wrong direction for
three rounds. It requires *decorrelation*, and the project has been generating
members by re-rendering prompts and fine-tuning descendants, both of which
produce correlated errors:

| way of adding a member | correlation with the trio | effect on the ensemble |
|---|---|---|
| same family, new checkpoint (round 119) | phi +1.000 | **-5.0 points** |
| `shadow` (accurate, errors aligned) | phi +0.095 | -0.4 points at matched coverage |
| `rules` (less accurate, decorrelated) | phi -0.014 | **+3.2 points at matched coverage** |

The next member should be chosen for its disagreement with the existing ones,
not for its solo accuracy - and round 119's admission-time test (does the
candidate disagree with an admitted lineage at all?) is the cheap version of
that metric, with this round's divergence table as the fuller one.
